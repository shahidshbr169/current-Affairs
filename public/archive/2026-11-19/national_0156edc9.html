SC questions use of Article 32 by homebuyers in stalled housing projects

19 November 2026•2 min read

Core Summary: The Supreme Court has questioned the propriety of invoking Article 32 of the Constitution by homebuyers in disputes with builders over stalled projects, and has referred the matter to a commercial court to examine the State Bank of India's recovery proceedings against buyers of a halted residential scheme in Sonipat.

UPSC Syllabus Mapping: GS Paper II – Governance, Constitution, Polity; GS Paper III – Economic Development (real‑estate sector & banking); Ethics in Public Administration (access to justice).

Key Data & Stats: Article 32 – Right to Constitutional Remedies, Commercial Courts Act, 2015, RERA, 2016, Sonipat, Haryana (stalled project), SBI recovery action

Deep-Dive Analysis

  • Background: The rapid expansion of the real‑estate sector post‑2000s, coupled with the 2016 Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act (RERA), aimed to protect homebuyers. However, a spate of project delays and defaults has led buyers to resort to litigation. Traditionally, disputes were heard under civil courts; some buyers have invoked Article 32 to approach the Supreme Court directly, arguing violation of fundamental rights.
  • Significance: The SC’s intervention underscores the tension between safeguarding fundamental rights and preserving the hierarchical judicial process. Direct petitions under Article 32 can bypass lower‑court mechanisms, potentially overburdening the apex court and diluting the role of specialized forums like commercial courts and RERA tribunals.
  • Challenges / Critical Analysis:
    • Jurisdictional ambiguity – whether a civil dispute over a contractual breach qualifies as a violation of a fundamental right.
    • Potential misuse of Article 32, leading to increased pendency in the Supreme Court.
    • Banking sector concerns – SBI’s recovery action reflects the tension between financial institutions’ rights and consumer protection.
    • Lack of a dedicated housing‑dispute mechanism despite RERA’s provisions.
  • Way Forward:
    • Amend the Civil Procedure Code to mandate exhaustion of remedies before invoking Article 32 in commercial disputes.
    • Strengthen RERA tribunals and fast‑track commercial courts for housing‑related cases, ensuring they have jurisdiction over recovery actions.
    • Set up a specialised “Housing Dispute Redressal Tribunal” under the aegis of the Ministry of Housing & Urban Affairs, possibly with a composition recommended by a committee akin to the Arunachal Pradesh Committee (ARC) on judicial reforms.
    • Encourage mediation and arbitration under the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, to reduce litigation burden.

Prelims Pointers

  • Article 32 – Right to move the Supreme Court for enforcement of fundamental rights.
  • Commercial Courts Act, 2015 – Provides for establishment of commercial courts for speedy resolution of commercial disputes.
  • Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act, 2016 (RERA) – Institutional framework for buyer protection and project registration.
  • Supreme Court’s stance on jurisdictional hierarchy in civil/commercial matters.
