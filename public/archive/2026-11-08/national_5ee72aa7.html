Congress donations fall nearly 75%, sharpest decline in a decade

8 November 2026•2 min read

Core Summary: The Indian National Congress reported a sharp fall of almost 75% in its electoral donations for FY 2024‑25, the steepest decline in a decade, with total contributions amounting to ₹517.39 crore. The dip signals mounting financial stress for a major national party and raises questions on the effectiveness of existing political funding regulations.

UPSC Syllabus Mapping: GS Paper II – Governance, Constitution, Polity (Political parties, Election Commission, political funding); GS Paper III – Security & International Relations (implications for political stability).

Key Data & Stats: ₹517.39 crore (2024‑25 contributions), ~75% decline, Sharpest drop in a decade, Election Commission of India (ECI) filing

Deep-Dive Analysis

  • Background: Political parties in India are mandated to disclose donations above ₹20,000 under the Representation of the People Act, 1951. The Supreme Court’s 2013 judgment directed the Election Commission to enforce stricter disclosure norms, prompting parties to file annual contribution reports.
  • Significance: A steep fall in donations affects a party’s ability to fund election campaigns, grassroots mobilisation, and policy research, potentially altering the competitive balance in electoral politics. It also reflects voter sentiment and donor confidence in the party’s relevance.
  • Challenges / Critical Analysis:
    • Opaque channels of indirect funding (e.g., corporate sponsorships, shell entities) remain difficult to trace.
    • Absence of a cap on individual or corporate donations creates a reliance on large donors, raising concerns of undue influence.
    • Enforcement limitations of the ECI and delayed audit mechanisms hinder real‑time monitoring.
  • Way Forward:
    • Strengthen the ECI’s audit capacity and introduce mandatory real‑time reporting of donations above ₹2 lakh.
    • Adopt the Law Commission’s recommendation for a ceiling on individual and corporate contributions (e.g., ₹5 crore per donor per election).
    • Consider a limited public funding scheme for recognised parties to reduce dependence on private money, subject to performance‑based criteria.
    • Set up an independent “Political Funding Oversight Committee” (akin to the ARC) to review compliance annually.

  • Supreme Court judgment (2013) on political funding transparency.
  • Article 324 of the Constitution – superintendence of elections.
