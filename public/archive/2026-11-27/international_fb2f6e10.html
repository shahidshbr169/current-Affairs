Is stubble burning in Punjab affecting Sri Lanka’s air quality?

27 November 2026•1 min read

Core Summary: Seasonal crop‑stubble burning in Punjab releases pollutants that are carried by the northeast monsoon across the Bay of Bengal, contributing to deteriorating air quality in Sri Lanka. The episode underscores the trans‑regional nature of atmospheric pollution in South Asia.

UPSC Syllabus Mapping: GS Paper II – International relations – Regional and global groupings involving India; environmental diplomacy; trans‑boundary pollution and climate change commitments.

Key Data & Agreements: UN Convention on Transboundary Air Pollution (1979), Paris Agreement (2015), Northeast monsoon (Oct‑Dec)

Deep-Dive Analysis

  • Context & Geopolitical Impact: The cross‑border drift of pollutants highlights the need for cooperative air‑quality management in South Asia, influencing India's standing in regional forums such as SAARC and BIMSTEC and shaping its climate‑leadership narrative.
  • Impact on India: International scrutiny over stubble burning may affect India's environmental credibility, affect trade‑related environmental clauses, and compel stricter domestic enforcement of the Air (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1981.
  • Way Forward: Strengthen bilateral mechanisms with Sri Lanka (e.g., MoU on air‑quality monitoring), promote clean‑crop‑residue technologies under the Pradhan Mantri Kisan Samman Nidhi (PM‑Kisan), and push for a South‑Asian trans‑boundary pollution protocol under the UN framework.

Prelims Pointers

  • Trans‑boundary air pollution falls under the UN Convention on Transboundary Air Pollution (1979).
  • Relevant regional groupings: SAARC, BIMSTEC, and the Indo‑Pacific environmental dialogue.
  • Seasonal vector: Northeast monsoon (Oct‑Dec) transports pollutants from northern India to Sri Lanka.
  • India’s climate commitments: Paris Agreement (2015) obliges mitigation of cross‑border emissions.
